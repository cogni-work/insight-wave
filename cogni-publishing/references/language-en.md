# English Executive Prose

Apply only for English output, at Phase 4 Pass 3, after evidence and argument are stable, and on top of `language-shared.md`.

## Idiom

- Prefer direct subject–verb constructions when agency matters: "the regulator set the deadline", not "a deadline was set by the regulator".
- Avoid stacked abstract nouns when a verb can state the action: "reduce downtime", not "the achievement of downtime reduction".
- Prefer concrete executive language over inflated consulting jargon: "cut", "fell", "costs", "decide" over "drive", "impact", "unlock", "operationalize".
- Keep idiomatic English information order — subject, verb, object, then circumstance — rather than importing another language's syntax; a German-sourced draft tends to front-load circumstance and hold the verb back.
- Preserve precise source and domain terminology; translate only what the brief's audience would not recognize.

## Cadence

- An English executive paragraph opens on its claim and closes on its consequence; the evidence sits between.
- Use punctuation to clarify hierarchy, not manufacture drama: colons introduce, semicolons balance two related clauses, dashes set off one aside at most per sentence. No exclamation marks.
- Numbers read as figures, with units and currency stated once and consistently: "€1.2M", "11 percent", "Q1 2027".

## Failure modes

- **Consultant fog** — "leverage synergies to drive transformative outcomes". Name the object, the actor and the outcome.
- **Hedge stacking** — "may potentially", "could arguably", "in some cases might". One qualifier carries the uncertainty the evidence supports; the rest is noise.
- **Nominal drift** — three abstract nouns in a row ("capability development prioritization"). Turn one into a verb.
- **Template cadence** — every paragraph the same length, every section opening with "In today's …". Vary, and cut the throat-clearing.
- **Anglo-American idiom in a European narrative** — "move the needle", "boil the ocean", "table this". Plain English travels; idiom does not.
